# Concept, Theme, and Emotion Review

Use this reference when the review concerns the identity of the game: its central promise, player fantasy, imaginative space, thematic point of view, emotional shape, expressive style, novelty, curiosity, or memorable qualities.

## Evidence to collect

- The shortest current pitch, intended audience, player role, repeated actions, target emotions, and the fantasy of being or doing that the game offers;
- visual, audio, motion, camera, writing, world, interface, and rules references actually guiding production;
- prototype footage, player descriptions, remembered moments, comparisons to other games, and points of surprise or confusion;
- style constraints, asset throughput, platform limits, and the cost of reproducing the intended presentation consistently.

## Review prompts

### Concept clarity

- Can the design state what the player repeatedly does, why it matters, and what makes that experience distinctive?
- Which mechanic or interaction proves the promise instead of merely describing it?
- If progression rewards and content volume were removed, what interesting activity would remain?

### Fantasy and imaginative space

- Who can players imagine themselves becoming here, and what can they do that is unavailable, difficult, or socially constrained elsewhere?
- Which simplifications make the world easier to understand and act within without making it feel arbitrary or shallow?
- What unusual capability creates freedom or wish fulfillment while leaving enough resistance for choices to matter?
- Which details must be concrete for rules and atmosphere to work, and which are stronger when players complete them through imagination?
- Does the game leave room for players to express a version of the fantasy that feels personally theirs?

### Thematic coherence

- What human question, value, conflict, or point of view gives the game direction?
- How do rules, costs, rewards, failure, and player freedom express that direction?
- Which subsystem or presentation choice sends a meaning that conflicts with the rest of the game?

### Emotional causality

- Which player decisions and game responses are intended to produce each important emotion?
- Does the experience create contrast between tension, relief, confidence, doubt, wonder, intimacy, or power rather than holding one tone continuously?
- Are emotions earned through participation, or asserted by dialogue, music, and spectacle after the fact?

### Expressive unity

- What shared shape, color, motion, sound, language, material, or timing principles make the game recognizable?
- Does each medium contribute a distinct part of the experience, or are some layers compensating for weak mechanics or unclear information?
- Can the team reproduce the style at the required content volume without losing readability or consistency?

### Atmosphere and sensory identity

- What does the environment communicate through light, space, ambient motion, sound, silence, texture, and density before any explicit explanation?
- Does the atmosphere deepen the intended action and emotion while keeping routes, hazards, characters, and feedback readable?
- How does the sensory identity change across locations and states without making them feel unrelated?

### Curiosity and discovery

- What question enters the player's mind early, and what gives them reason to pursue it?
- Does each answer create a useful new question, choice, or interpretation?
- Are mysteries supported by discoverable evidence, or maintained by withholding information the player has already earned?

### Novelty and surprise

- Which part is meaningfully unfamiliar, and which familiar structure helps players understand it?
- Does novelty continue to develop after the opening, or disappear once the premise is known?
- Can a surprising result make sense in hindsight and teach the player something usable?

### Memorable identity

- Which interaction, decision, image, sound, or story generated by play would players describe to another person?
- Is that moment representative of normal play or an isolated promotional scene?
- Which borrowed conventions are necessary for readability, and where does the design have its own voice?

## Failure signals

- the pitch describes setting and features but not a compelling repeated activity;
- the advertised fantasy is visible in art and story but unavailable through player action;
- power removes resistance so thoroughly that the fantasy stops producing decisions;
- exhaustive explanation leaves no room for interpretation, while missing rule detail is mistaken for mystery;
- theme exists in dialogue and art while rewards encourage the opposite behavior;
- emotional goals have no identifiable rule, choice, or feedback cause;
- the style is attractive in isolated assets but inconsistent in motion or actual gameplay;
- atmosphere depends on heavy presentation that obscures important gameplay information;
- mystery depends on arbitrary concealment rather than interpretation;
- novelty is concentrated in terminology while interaction remains conventional;
- the most memorable moment is unrelated to the core loop.

## Small experiments

- Present the concept without genre labels and ask target players what they expect to do and feel.
- Build the smallest version of the signature interaction with placeholder presentation and test whether its identity survives.
- Create a theme-to-system map showing how each major rule reinforces, ignores, or contradicts the intended point of view.
- Show one sequence with alternate sound, timing, or visual treatments and compare emotional interpretation and gameplay readability.
- Test one location with presentation layers added in stages and identify when atmosphere improves, stops helping, or begins to hide information.
- Ask players what question they most wanted answered, what surprised them, and which moment they would retell.
- Ask players to complete “I am someone who can...” after playing, then compare their answers with the intended fantasy and actual mechanics.
- Remove one explanatory detail and one mechanical detail separately; test which omission invites imagination and which only creates confusion.
