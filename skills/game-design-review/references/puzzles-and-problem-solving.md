# Puzzles and Problem-Solving Review

Use this reference for explicit puzzles and for any gameplay segment that asks players to understand a state, form a hypothesis, test an action, and use the result to make progress.

## Evidence to collect

- Initial state, goal state, available actions, constraints, information, feedback, failure cost, and reset behavior;
- intended reasoning steps, alternate solutions, dead ends, optional discoveries, hint stages, and prerequisite knowledge;
- solve times, first actions, repeated ineffective actions, hint use, abandonment points, and player explanations;
- how the problem is introduced, represented, escalated, and connected to surrounding mechanics, world, and pacing.

## Review prompts

### Entry and representation

- Can players identify something useful to inspect or try before they understand the full solution?
- Are objects, relationships, constraints, and state changes represented consistently?
- Does the puzzle require information that was never available or taught?

### Reasoning chain

- What observation supports each intended inference?
- Can players test a hypothesis and receive feedback that narrows the solution space?
- Does success come from understanding a relationship, or from exhaustively trying combinations?

### Progress and recovery

- What intermediate change tells players they are closer to the goal?
- Can an incorrect action produce information without causing excessive repetition?
- Can players recognize and recover from an invalid state, or must they restart without knowing why?

### Solution structure

- Is there one hard bottleneck that stops all progress, and would a parallel problem or optional path preserve momentum?
- Do alternate solutions reflect different insights or tools rather than accidental exploits?
- Does the final step combine earlier discoveries into a satisfying conclusion?

### Hint escalation

- Does the first hint redirect attention, the next clarify a relationship, and only the final hint reveal an operation?
- Can players request help without losing more autonomy than necessary?
- Does the game distinguish missing observation, misunderstood rule, forgotten information, and execution difficulty?

### Integration and variety

- Does the problem use rules the rest of the game values?
- Does its fiction and environment help represent the logic rather than disguising it arbitrarily?
- Do consecutive puzzles vary the kind of reasoning, information, and action required?

## Failure signals

- players cannot form a first hypothesis from the visible state;
- progress is hidden until the entire solution is complete;
- brute force is faster or safer than reasoning;
- one missed detail blocks every available path;
- hints repeat the goal instead of addressing the player's actual misunderstanding;
- reset cost punishes experimentation unrelated to the failed inference;
- the puzzle teaches a rule that never matters elsewhere.

## Small experiments

- Observe the first three actions without helping and ask players to explain their current hypothesis after each.
- Expose one intermediate state change and test whether players can now locate the next reasoning step.
- Replace a full-solution hint with staged attention, relationship, and operation hints; record where help becomes effective.
- Provide a parallel subproblem during the main bottleneck and compare abandonment and return behavior.
- Ask successful players to reconstruct why the solution works; distinguish understanding from remembered input sequence.
