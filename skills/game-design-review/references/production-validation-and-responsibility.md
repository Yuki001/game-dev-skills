# Production, Validation, and Responsibility Review

Use this reference for scope, prototypes, playtests, evidence quality, stakeholder alignment, documentation, team collaboration, technology choices, market position, acquisition, distribution, commercial sustainability, project purpose, and player welfare.

## Evidence to collect

- Team capabilities, schedule, budget, dependencies, content volume, platform requirements, and operational obligations;
- the riskiest design assumptions, prototype history, test participants, observation methods, and decision records;
- stakeholder requests, actual success criteria, pitch claims, document owners, decision rights, and unresolved disagreements;
- technology constraints that directly enable or limit the intended experience;
- competing alternatives, audience discovery and acquisition channels, production, marketing, distribution, platform, support, and maintenance costs;
- monetization, retention, moderation, privacy, rating, ongoing-support expectations, and intended positive player outcomes where relevant.

## Review prompts

### Scope integrity

- Which features are necessary to validate the core promise?
- Which content volume assumptions have real production estimates behind them?
- What can be removed while preserving the intended player decision and experience?

### Risk retirement

- Which unresolved assumption could invalidate the project or cause the most expensive rework?
- What is the cheapest artifact that can expose that risk?
- Is the team building breadth before proving the hardest interaction or production pipeline?

### Evidence quality

- Does the current evidence come from intended players performing representative tasks?
- Are observations separated from interpretation and preference?
- What result would cause the team to reverse or abandon the current decision?

### Stakeholder fit and pitch

- What does each stakeholder request, what outcome do they actually need, and how will they decide whether the project succeeded?
- Which pitch claim is demonstrated by evidence, which is a hypothesis, and which depends on future production scale?
- Does the proposal explain why this audience, experience, scope, and business shape belong together?

### Communication and documentation

- What must be remembered, decided, handed off, or kept consistent for production to continue?
- Is each document serving a named reader and decision, or being maintained because the format is customary?
- Can a new contributor distinguish current decisions, rejected alternatives, assumptions, and open questions?

### Team alignment

- Does the team share the same player promise and current priority?
- Who owns each decision, who supplies evidence, and who must be consulted before it changes?
- Can disagreement be converted into competing hypotheses and a test rather than authority or endless preference debate?

### Technology fit

- Which part of the player experience genuinely depends on the proposed technology?
- Has the team validated performance, tooling, content throughput, deployment, and maintenance—not only a visual demo?
- Is technical novelty consuming the iteration budget needed for design learning?

### Forecasting and resilience

- Which platform, audience, cost, regulation, dependency, or technology assumption is most likely to change during production or operation?
- Which design commitments are difficult to reverse if that forecast is wrong?
- Can the project preserve its central experience under a smaller budget, slower content cadence, lost dependency, or different platform condition?

### Market and distribution fit

- What existing games, entertainment, hobbies, or tools compete for the same audience need, time, attention, and spending?
- Why would the intended audience discover, understand, trust, and choose this game over those alternatives?
- Do production, marketing, localization, distribution, platform, customer-support, moderation, infrastructure, and maintenance costs fit the expected revenue and lifespan?
- Which platform rules, storefront dynamics, hardware requirements, regional conditions, or discovery channels constrain the design and launch plan?
- What is difficult for a competitor to reproduce: the mechanic, content pipeline, community, brand, expertise, technology, or accumulated player investment?

### Sustainability

- Do price, monetization, retention, and content cadence expectations support the actual production and operating cost?
- Does revenue depend on confusion, pressure, concealed odds, social obligation, or impaired exit decisions?
- What happens to player access, progress, and communities if ongoing service stops?

### Responsibility

- Which players could be excluded, pressured, misled, exposed, or harmed by the design?
- What data, communication, spending, or play patterns require stronger defaults and recovery paths?
- Does the benefit of the feature justify its foreseeable misuse and operational burden?

### Positive impact and purpose

- What capability, understanding, relationship, emotion, or perspective should players carry away from the experience?
- Which repeated action and consequence could create that outcome, and what could create the opposite outcome?
- Is pursuing this project and feature worth the team time, player attention, and ongoing obligation it requires?

## Failure signals

- milestones count features rather than answer risks;
- prototypes demonstrate appearance but not the core decision or content pipeline;
- playtests collect satisfaction scores without observing behavior;
- stakeholders agree on features but disagree on what success means;
- documents describe the product but do not preserve decisions, evidence, ownership, or change history;
- unresolved team disagreements repeatedly reappear because no test or decision owner exists;
- technology is selected for novelty without a player-facing dependency;
- the plan assumes stable platforms, costs, tools, or content throughput without a fallback;
- the audience is defined, but no credible path exists for reaching and convincing it;
- projections omit marketing, distribution, support, moderation, infrastructure, or long-term maintenance costs;
- differentiation depends on a feature competitors can reproduce before launch;
- the design requires a platform, price, region, or acquisition channel whose constraints it has not validated;
- the business model requires a cadence the team cannot sustain;
- safety, moderation, accessibility, privacy, or shutdown planning is deferred until launch;
- claimed positive impact has no connection to repeated player behavior.

## Small experiments

- Build a risk register ordered by impact, uncertainty, and cost of learning; prototype the top item only.
- Run a concierge or paper test before automating a costly system.
- Predefine a decision threshold, then conduct a small representative playtest and record whether the threshold was met.
- Estimate one complete content unit from concept through integration and validation before committing to total volume.
- Conduct a misuse and failure workshop for one social, spending, or data feature and assign mitigations to concrete owners.
- Ask each stakeholder to independently define success and compare incompatible assumptions before revising scope.
- Remove or shorten one document and test whether any real decision, handoff, or consistency check becomes harder.
- Convert one team disagreement into two observable predictions and run the cheapest test that separates them.
- Run a reduced-budget or lost-dependency scenario and identify which parts of the player promise survive.
- Define one intended positive player outcome and one plausible adverse outcome, then design observations for both.
- Compare the game against the alternatives players currently choose, using the same audience need rather than genre labels alone.
- Build a full-lifecycle cost model from development through discovery, distribution, support, operation, and shutdown; identify the assumption with the largest effect.
- Test the pitch and acquisition path with representative players who are not already following the project.
