# Actions, Rules, and Agency Review

Use this reference for core mechanics, goal structure, tactical or strategic decisions, performance judgment, information, rule clarity, complexity, and player control.

## Evidence to collect

- Player verbs and the objects or situations each verb affects;
- short-term goals, medium-term goals, long-term goals, player-defined goals, constraints, state changes, information visibility, costs, rewards, and failure rules;
- observed choices, repeated sequences, ignored actions, dominant strategies, and unintended exploits;
- the difference between rules as written, rules as implemented, and rules players believe.

## Review prompts

### Decision shape

- What information is available when the player must commit?
- Which options express genuinely different priorities rather than different numeric efficiency?
- Can the player explain why a good outcome followed from the choice?

### Goal architecture

- What is the immediate actionable goal, what larger goal gives it meaning, and how can players see the relationship between them?
- Are short-term goals, medium-term goals, and long-term goals balanced so players can make progress without losing direction?
- Where can players set, select, or reinterpret goals of their own without undermining the game's central promise?
- Do simultaneous goals create useful prioritization, or merely force players to track unrelated obligations?

### Agency and consequence

- Which outcomes are controlled by preparation, execution, adaptation, and chance?
- Does the player have a way to recognize and recover from a bad decision?
- Are important consequences delayed so far that learning becomes unreliable?

### Performance judgment

- What ability or understanding is the game claiming to evaluate when it rewards, ranks, passes, or fails a player?
- Does the result distinguish that ability from time investment, prior unlocks, hidden information, device advantage, or random variance?
- Is the basis of judgment visible enough for players to consider the result legitimate and know how to improve?
- Does the game judge players in a way the intended audience actually values?

### Rule pressure

- What behavior does each reward, cost, limit, and exception encourage?
- Does any local incentive undermine the larger intended play style?
- Which rule exists only to repair a problem introduced by another rule?

### Strategic health

- Is one option consistently safest, easiest, and most rewarding?
- Can players pursue more than one viable plan without pretending obviously weaker choices are equal?
- Do new combinations create interesting consequences or merely more cases to memorize?

### Complexity and systemic character

- Which complexity is written directly into rules, and which emerges from interactions between a smaller set of rules?
- Can an element serve several coherent purposes, or can a single-purpose element be removed or combined?
- Do unusual edge cases create recognizable character and stories, or only inconsistency and maintenance cost?
- Has simplification removed meaningful decisions, or only reduced explanation and bookkeeping?

### Uncertainty

- Does uncertainty create anticipation and adaptation, or obscure causality?
- Can players influence exposure to risk before the random result occurs?
- Are displayed probabilities and outcome ranges consistent with player experience?

## Failure signals

- Correct play becomes a fixed script;
- choices differ visually but converge on the same outcome;
- local goals remain clear while the larger purpose becomes invisible, or long-term goals provide no useful next action;
- the game presents a score or rank without clearly judging a skill players can improve;
- failure feels arbitrary because causes are hidden or feedback arrives too late;
- exceptions outnumber the rules players can reason from;
- complexity comes mainly from memorizing special cases rather than combining understandable rules;
- every element has one narrow function, making the system larger without making it deeper;
- randomness erases meaningful preparation or execution;
- the game offers freedom but punishes deviation from an undisclosed intended path.

## Small experiments

- Remove the suspected dominant option and observe whether decision variety improves or a deeper dominant pattern appears.
- Reveal one hidden state or probability and test whether player predictions become more accurate.
- Run short scenarios with identical goals but different resource pressure to see whether strategies actually change.
- Ask players to predict the next result before it resolves; compare prediction errors with the intended uncertainty.
- Ask players at several moments to state their immediate and larger goals; locate where the hierarchy stops being understood.
- Compare the game's ranking with direct observation of the ability it claims to measure; identify unrelated variables influencing the result.
- Remove or merge one single-purpose rule and test whether decision variety, clarity, or systemic character is actually lost.
