# Narrative, World, and Character Review

Use this reference for authored story, emergent stories, quests, dialogue, worldbuilding, characters, player role, choice, and environmental delivery.

## Evidence to collect

- Player role, embodiment, core verbs, premise, stakes, themes, character motivations, relationships, world rules, and story structure;
- delivery channels such as action, environment, dialogue, quest state, UI, item text, cutscene, and systemic events;
- branch counts, content budget, skipped content, comprehension, remembered moments, and player retellings;
- contradictions between gameplay incentives, character claims, world logic, and intended tone.

## Review prompts

### Playable causality

- What does the player do that causes the next narrative state?
- Does the story recognize actions the game repeatedly encourages?
- Where is the player waiting for authored events instead of participating in them?

### Role and motivation

- What does the player character want, and what does the player controlling them want?
- Are those motivations aligned, productively tense, or accidentally contradictory?
- Does the role explain why the player has the game's central abilities and responsibilities?

### Player embodiment

- What identity, body, position, or perspective connects the player to the world?
- Which traits are fixed to make the role meaningful, and which remain open for player projection or expression?
- Do movement, camera, scale, capability, vulnerability, and social response reinforce the intended embodiment?

### World integrity

- Which world rules create everyday consequences in locations, resources, institutions, and behavior?
- Does gameplay routinely violate a rule the story treats as important?
- Which lore exists only as exposition and could instead shape a decision or space?

### Character function

- What pressure, information, relationship, or change does each major character introduce?
- Do characters demonstrate their traits through choices and behavior rather than description alone?
- Can multiple characters be combined without losing a distinct dramatic or gameplay function?

### Relationship structure

- How does each important relationship differ in trust, dependence, conflict, intimacy, obligation, or power?
- Which relationships change because of player actions, and how is that change made visible?
- Are there missing or duplicated relationship functions that make the cast feel narrow or redundant?

### Character change

- What belief, strategy, loyalty, status, or self-understanding changes for each central character?
- Which events and choices make that change plausible rather than merely announced?
- Does the player's influence on a transformation match the agency promised by the game?

### Choice and consequence

- What information supports a narrative choice, and what uncertainty is intentional?
- Is the consequence visible at a scale appropriate to the promise made by the choice?
- Does branching create meaning proportional to its production and consistency cost?

## Failure signals

- the story praises behavior that the mechanics discourage;
- player choices change dialogue but not relationships, goals, access, or later situations despite promising consequence;
- worldbuilding adds names and history without affecting play;
- characters deliver information but exert no pressure on decisions;
- the player role promises self-expression while embodiment and dialogue force one unexplained identity;
- relationships remain static despite events that should alter trust or power;
- a character changes abruptly because the plot needs a new function;
- required exposition arrives during high-attention gameplay;
- branching multiplies content while producing nearly identical player experiences.

## Small experiments

- Remove explanatory text from one sequence and test what players infer from action and environment.
- Convert one story beat into a player decision and compare comprehension, agency, and production cost.
- Ask players to retell what happened and why; separate missed facts from missing causality.
- Map each major character to a unique pressure or function and test whether any role is redundant.
- Draw a relationship map before and after a major beat; verify that intended changes appear in behavior, access, dialogue, or mechanics.
- Prototype one important scene with two different degrees of avatar definition and compare role clarity with player projection.
