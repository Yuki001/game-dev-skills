# Space, Level, and Pacing Review

Use this reference for level layouts, exploration, encounters, navigation, checkpoints, spatial readability, tension, rest, and sequence rhythm.

## Evidence to collect

- Maps, critical path, optional routes, landmarks, sightlines, encounter boundaries, traversal times, and checkpoints;
- player traces, wrong turns, deaths, pauses, camera problems, and points where players miss important content;
- intended pacing beats and actual time spent in combat, traversal, choice, recovery, narrative, and menus;
- movement capabilities, camera limits, interaction range, enemy perception, and platform constraints.

## Review prompts

### Spatial purpose

- What decision or feeling is each area designed to support?
- Does geometry create that behavior, or does the design rely on markers and instructions to compensate?
- Which spaces can be removed or combined without losing a distinct function?

### Navigation and readability

- Can players identify a near-term destination and a way to make progress toward it?
- Do landmarks remain useful from the angles and distances at which choices occur?
- Are optional routes visibly optional without revealing every outcome?

### Encounter structure

- What information can players gather before commitment?
- How do cover, elevation, distance, chokepoints, hazards, and escape routes alter viable tactics?
- Does the space support the abilities and enemy behaviors the encounter expects?

### Rhythm and recovery

- Where does pressure rise, peak, release, and rebuild?
- Is downtime giving players recovery, planning, discovery, or only travel time?
- Does failure return players to a useful learning opportunity or force repetition unrelated to the mistake?

### Sequence variation

- Are consecutive beats demanding the same attention and skill?
- Does a later area transform an established spatial idea rather than merely enlarging it?
- Can optional content adjust rhythm for different player motivations without breaking the main path?

## Failure signals

- wayfinding depends on UI markers because the space itself gives no usable structure;
- combat arenas look different but produce the same positioning pattern;
- checkpoints minimize distance but maximize repeated setup or dialogue;
- long quiet sections contain neither choice nor anticipation;
- a camera or control limitation conflicts with required spatial awareness;
- spectacle obscures the route or hazard at the moment of decision.

## Small experiments

- Test an untextured greybox with navigation markers disabled and record decision points rather than only completion time.
- Move one landmark, route opening, or checkpoint and compare wrong turns and repeated traversal.
- Run the same encounter in two layouts to separate enemy-rule problems from space problems.
- Create a beat timeline from recorded play and compare intended and observed pressure, rest, and decision density.
