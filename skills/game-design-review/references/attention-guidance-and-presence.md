# Attention, Guidance, and Presence Review

Use this reference when the design problem concerns sustained interest, memorable moments, player focus, perceived freedom, nonverbal guidance, immersion, identification, or stories players create and retell through play.

## Evidence to collect

- A timeline of goals, decisions, challenges, reveals, rests, reversals, rewards, interruptions, and climaxes;
- recorded play, gaze or camera direction where available, hesitation, skipped content, menu time, drop-off, and retold moments;
- level composition, goals, characters, lighting, animation, sound, music, UI, and reward placement used to guide attention;
- player expectations about their role, the world's responsiveness, and which events they believe they caused.

## Review prompts

### Attention curve

- What creates immediate orientation and interest without exhausting the strongest material?
- How do uncertainty, difficulty, consequence, novelty, and emotional stakes change over time?
- Does each peak earn its intensity through setup, and does the experience provide enough recovery to make the next rise perceptible?

### Moments and contrast

- Which moments should players anticipate, recognize while they happen, and remember afterward?
- What quieter or simpler beat gives an important moment contrast?
- Are memorable events created by player commitment and consequence, or merely shown to the player?

### Voluntary guidance

- What action would produce the best next experience, and what makes the player want to choose it?
- Can goals, geometry, motion, characters, sound, lighting, resource placement, and consequence point in the same direction without removing agency?
- Does guidance remain legible when players approach from an unexpected route or ignore the first cue?

### Freedom and overload

- Where does freedom create ownership, and where does it create uncertainty without meaningful difference?
- Which constraints make decisions clearer, and which expose the author's preferred path too strongly?
- Can players postpone one objective, pursue another, and later recover context?

### Presence and continuity

- Which interface, loading, camera, animation, rule exception, or narrative transition reminds players of the machinery instead of the world and role?
- Does the world respond consistently enough for players to trust their expectations?
- Are interruptions necessary, timed well, and presented through a channel that fits the current attention demand?

### Identification and retelling

- What parts of the role leave room for players to project their own priorities or style?
- Which combinations of rules allow outcomes the designer did not script but players can still understand?
- Can a player explain not only what happened, but what they decided and why the outcome felt personal?

## Failure signals

- the opening peaks before players understand what matters;
- every beat demands maximum attention, flattening the perceived intensity;
- objectives, level composition, characters, and rewards pull in different directions;
- freedom means many destinations but few distinct consequences;
- frequent noninteractive interruptions break action and context;
- scripted spectacle receives more emphasis than player-caused turning points;
- players retell the authored plot but cannot describe a decision that became their story.

## Small experiments

- Plot an observed session as attention demand and reported interest; compare it with the intended curve.
- Remove one explicit marker and strengthen environmental, character, or reward guidance; measure route choice and hesitation.
- Let players defer a main objective, then test whether they can recover purpose after optional activity.
- Compare a scripted event with a mechanically caused version and ask which felt more personal and memorable.
- Ask players to retell the session using first-person decisions; identify where causality or ownership disappears.
