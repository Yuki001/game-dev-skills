# Challenge, Progression, and Economy Review

Use this reference for difficulty, rewards, growth, currencies, itemization, long-term goals, replay depth, and balance between viable paths.

## Evidence to collect

- Challenge variables, expected player skill growth, failure frequency, recovery time, and difficulty spikes;
- progression unlocks, power curves, reward schedule, resource sources, sinks, stockpiles, and exchange rules;
- completion, churn, purchase, build-selection, and resource-balance data where available;
- intended differences between novice, practiced, and expert play.

## Review prompts

### Challenge curve

- Which skill or understanding is each challenge testing?
- Does the game increase complexity, execution demand, uncertainty, or punishment, and is that the intended dimension?
- Where can players practice a requirement before it becomes a hard gate?

### Progression meaning

- Does an unlock create a new decision, expand expression, or merely increase numbers?
- Can players see a credible path to the next meaningful change?
- Does progression preserve room for mastery, or replace it with accumulated power?

### Reward integrity

- What player behavior earns each reward?
- Does the reward reinforce the desired behavior or encourage repetition detached from the core experience?
- Are rewards understandable soon enough to influence decisions?

### Economy health

- For each resource, where is it created, stored, converted, lost, and consumed?
- What prevents permanent surplus, irreversible poverty, or one compulsory purchase order?
- Are prices meaningful relative to opportunity cost rather than arbitrary scale?

### Build and content viability

- Which options remain useful across the actual content mix?
- Is an option weak because of its numbers, because its situation rarely occurs, or because its value is hard to perceive?
- Can players recover from an uninformed early commitment without erasing commitment entirely?

## Failure signals

- difficulty rises mainly by increasing health or reducing tolerance for error;
- rewards are frequent but stop changing player decisions;
- players hoard a resource because its future value is unknowable;
- one currency or build solves every category of problem;
- progression makes earlier mechanics irrelevant instead of deepening them;
- balancing targets averages that hide distinct novice and expert experiences.

## Small experiments

- Plot challenge demand and recovery opportunities over time, then compare the graph with observed failure clusters.
- Simulate resource balances for conservative, expected, and optimizing players.
- Offer two progression paths with equal total power but different decision patterns and compare selection and satisfaction.
- Temporarily improve communication of an underused option before changing its numbers; test whether usage changes.
